Why your heart rate stays high after exercise

You stop running, but your watch still shows a high heart rate for many minutes. That's normal, and it's actually a useful measure of how fit you are that day.

Updated October 12, 2026 · 5 min read

Two phases, two mechanisms

Physiologists describe your heart coming down in two stages. During the first minute, the drop is fast: it's mostly your "rest" nervous system (parasympathetic, through the vagus nerve) taking over again. Then comes a slow phase, when your "action" system (sympathetic) gradually backs off and your body clears the heat and by-products of the effort. Read the review

That's why the number falls quickly at first, then seems "stuck": the second phase takes time, especially after a long effort, in hot weather, or when you're dehydrated.

What slows the comedown

  • Heat and not drinking enough.
  • Stress: going straight to your email or a tense conversation.
  • Built-up fatigue, lack of sleep, an illness coming on.
  • Standing still and tense right after a fast finish.

What helps

  • A few minutes of walking rather than stopping abruptly.
  • Slow breathing, with an out-breath longer than the in-breath. A 2025 trial found a faster return to rest with six breaths a minute after exercise. Read the study
  • Drinking and getting somewhere cool.
  • Relaxing your shoulders, jaw and hands.

Reading what your watch says

Compare yourself with yourself, not with others: how fast your heart comes down depends on age, fitness, the session, and even the watch. If your heart comes down clearly less well than usual after a similar session, it's often a sign of fatigue. An easier day won't hurt.

When to see a doctor. Chest pain or tightness, unusual palpitations, unusual shortness of breath, feeling faint or dizzy and it doesn't pass: stop exercising and call a doctor, or your local emergency number (15 in France) in an emergency.
